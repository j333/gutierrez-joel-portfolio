## How does one team create on-brand content for every market?

After the acquisition of [GetGloby](/getgloby), I led product design at Marketfully: a web app for marketing teams that create content for global audiences. Translation, transcreation, and content creation in one product, with AI writing the first draft and people deciding what shipped.

<Facts>
  <Fact title="The research">Personas, the buying committee, and the finding that reshaped the product: the human could not be removed.</Fact>
  <Fact title="The concepts">Architecture, flows, and an agent stack drawn ahead of each release.</Fact>
  <Fact title="The platform">Workspace, brand, and a loop from opportunity to every market, with a person in the lead at every step.</Fact>
</Facts>

### Problem: global content was slow, inconsistent, and easy to get wrong.

Teams producing for several markets juggled briefs, drafts, translators, and reviewers across tools. Every handoff lost context, so the brand sounded different in each language and errors slipped through. Generic AI tools made drafting faster without making it safer: they wrote from nothing, with no brand, no audience, and no brief to hold them.

### Opportunity: one platform, from the idea to every market.

If the brand lived in the product and every brief carried it, one team could go from an idea to a finished asset in every market without losing the thread. AI could draft, people could decide, and native experts could step in only where a model fell short.

<Facts>
  <Fact title="Shared context">Brand, audience, and glossary travelled with every brief and every asset it produced.</Fact>
  <Fact title="AI first draft">Generation gave the team something to shape, not something to accept.</Fact>
  <Fact title="Human in the lead">Reviewers and native experts stepped in where a model should not be trusted.</Fact>
</Facts>

## What we expected from AI, and what we found

The bet going in, from the buyers as much as from us, was full automation: a platform that would take a brand and produce every asset for every market with no one in between. We spent the first months testing that bet. We mapped the problems teams actually had, ran the models against real briefs, and studied cases where generated content had already shipped.

The finding was consistent. Generation cut hours from most steps, and many of them ran on their own. But every piece that reached a market without a person guiding the production and checking each change came back wrong: off-tone, off-brand, or plainly incorrect. The human was not a fallback for when the model failed. It was the part of the system that made the rest worth using.

![Agentic AI concept diagram with a loop between human in the lead, agentic AI, and context or environment, fed by internal brand data and external channels, competitors, industry, and trends](/projects/marketfully/agentic-loop.webp#wide "The loop we drew after that finding: the person steers, the agent works from brand data and the outside context on a set cadence, and nothing publishes without feedback.")

The research also told us who was buying. Three people sat in every purchase: the champion who brought the product in, the economic buyer who paid for it, and the operational gatekeeper who had to live with it. We documented each one, with the buying signals and the tech stack around them, and designed for the three at once.

![Research board with persona cards for the champion, the economic buyer, and the operational gatekeeper, plus buying signals and tech stack](/projects/marketfully/personas.webp#wide "Personas and buying committee: the champion, the economic buyer, and the operational gatekeeper, with buying signals and tech stack.")

## A design system and a team to ship it

I built the design system the product shipped on. It started from a color palette, then semantic tokens for text, icons, surfaces, and borders, named by role, not by color, and those tokens became components. The components were applied in different templates, consistent across the platform and intuitive to use, so a critical border or a subdued label meant the same thing on every screen. The interface stayed quiet on purpose. The brands inside it had to be the loudest thing on the page.

![Design system tokens file with descriptive color tokens for text, icons, surfaces, tints, and borders, grouped by default, primary, critical, interactive, and state](/projects/marketfully/design-system.webp#wide "Tokens: descriptive colors for text, icons, surfaces, and borders, named by role so every screen reads the same way.")

I built and led the product design team: a Product Designer, an AI Solution Designer, and a UX Researcher, reporting to me. Together we owned how the product looked, how it behaved, and how we learned from the people buying and using it. I also stood up the AI team that turned those designs into agent-based features, working alongside product, engineering, and QA on every release.

![Release contributors slide listing development, product design, product management, and QA, beside a video call grid of the team](/projects/marketfully/team.webp#wide "One release, one team: development, product design, product management, and QA.")

## What shipped: a workspace that holds the team and the brand

Organizations held workspaces and teams. Admins created teams, gave each member a role such as account manager or member, and decided which teams and users could reach each workspace. One company could run marketing, sales, and HR side by side without splitting the brand across tools.

The brand lived in the product, not in a PDF. Content for Argentina and content for the United States came from the same source.

## From an opportunity to every market

Home offered four paths, each labeled with who did the work. Request expert services was human. Create a brief, create an asset, and transcreate content were AI and human together. Below them, content ideas suggested from the brand and its recent activity.

![Marketfully home with four creation paths labeled AI or human, and content suggested for a workspace](/projects/marketfully/home.webp#wide "Home: four paths, each labeled with who does the work, and content suggested from the brand's recent activity.")

Opportunities ranked content ideas by score, type, cost, market, and effort, and every row ended in the same action: start a brief. The brief held goal, audience, language and location, tone, and an outline per asset template. The assistant filled it in with the team, checked it for inconsistencies, and tracked how complete it was before anything was generated. The draft then opened in the editor beside the brief, its keywords, and a risk score.

Generation was a first draft. The team iterated it in place: improve, shorten, check keywords, adjust the tone, or rewrite, without leaving the document. When the piece needed a native eye, expert services took it: content review, accuracy assessment, video translation, or a custom request, each tracked with cost and status. Once an asset was approved it could be transcreated for other audiences, so one campaign reached every market without being rewritten from scratch in each language.

## What we were willing to trade

We traded the promise that sold the product for the one that held up. Full automation was easier to pitch. A person in the lead was what worked. So the agent drafts from brand data and the outside context, the team reviews each change, and only then does the asset go out. An asset that drifted from its brief was a failed asset, even if an expert could have fixed it later.

> We traded full automation for a person in the lead.

## What I learned

<Facts>
  <Fact title="Automation is not autonomy">The models cut the time in most steps, but someone still had to guide the production and check every change.</Fact>
  <Fact title="Document the brand once">When guidelines, tone, and glossaries live in the product, AI and people work from the same context.</Fact>
  <Fact title="Bind the asset to the brief">Generated work stays on-brand only if the brief is still attached when someone edits it.</Fact>
</Facts>
