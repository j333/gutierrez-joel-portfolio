## How does a team create on-brand content for global audiences?

After the acquisition of [GetGloby](/getgloby), I led product design at Marketfully: a web app for marketing teams that create content for global audiences. One product to translate, transcreate, and generate content, with AI in the workflow and native-language experts available for human review.

<Facts>
  <Fact title="The research">Experts, and the finding that reshaped the product: the human could not be removed from the process.</Fact>
  <Fact title="The concepts">Architecture, flows, and an agent harness with skills, tools, and models set up for each task.</Fact>
  <Fact title="The platform">Workspace, brand, and suggested opportunities and improvements for each market, with a human in the loop.</Fact>
</Facts>

### Problem: slow turnaround, inconsistency, and AI used the wrong way.

Working across tools and channels added complexity. Every handoff lost context, so the brand sounded different in each language and errors piled up. Generic AI tools produced drafts quickly, and without confidence: they wrote from a blank page, with no brand, no audience, and no brief to hold them. Fixing that became repetitive work, and the same team still shipped inconsistent content.

### Opportunity: one platform, from the idea to the value proposition.

If the brand lived in the product, and every brief was shared with the AI workflows and with the people doing the work, one team could go from an idea to a finished asset in every market without losing the thread. They could also request professional services from inside the platform. AI could flag likely problems, draft, and translate. People decided, and native-language experts stepped in when the risk or the budget called for it.

<Facts>
  <Fact title="Shared context">Brand, audience, and glossary live in every workspace and travel with every brief and every asset.</Fact>
  <Fact title="AI first draft">The first draft gave the team something to iterate, not something to accept.</Fact>
  <Fact title="Human in the lead">Writers, reviewers, and experts approved each stage and iterated together on the same piece.</Fact>
</Facts>

## What we expected from AI, and what we found

The starting expectation, from the people leading marketing teams and from our own team, was full automation: a platform that would take a brand and produce every asset for every market, with no one in between. We spent the first months testing that hypothesis. We mapped the problems marketing teams actually had, ran the models against real briefs, and studied cases where generated content had already published.

The finding was consistent. Automation saved hours on most steps, and many of them already ran on their own. But a piece that reached a market without a person guiding production and reviewing each stage was not always right, or worth reading: off tone, off brand, or simply irrelevant. The human was not a fallback for when the model missed. It was the part of the process that made the content worth publishing.

![Agentic AI concept diagram with a loop between human in the lead, agentic AI, and context or environment, fed by internal brand data and external channels, competitors, industry, and trends](/projects/marketfully/agentic-loop.webp#wide "The loop I designed after that finding: the person directs and defines, the agent works from brand data and outside context on a set cadence, and nothing publishes without human review and approval.")

The research also helped us define our users. Three people sat in every purchase: the champion, who brought the product in; the economic buyer, who paid for it; and the operational gatekeeper, who had to work in it every day. We documented each one, including buying signals and the tech stack they used.

## Concepts: the platform and its architecture

The v2 architecture I developed set the hierarchy as organization, workspace, brands, and content. The knowledge base (brand, voice and tone, glossaries, audience personas, style guides) was built into every workspace. That structure flexed across the use cases we had already identified.

![Platform architecture board with the organization, workspace, brands, and content hierarchy, five functional zones, and a knowledge base map](/projects/marketfully/workspace.webp#wide "Architecture v2: organization, workspace, brands, and content, with the knowledge base built into every workspace.")

The flow is not linear. It opens into opportunities and improvements, brief development, content generation, and transcreation for different audiences.

The system underneath has a few core layers: an API and MCP layer, an agent harness with tools, skills, and subagents, and a brand brain.

## A design system, and a team to build it

I developed the design system the product was built on. It started from a color palette, then semantic tokens for text, icons, surfaces, and borders, named by role rather than by color, and those tokens became components. Components were applied across templates, consistent on every screen and intuitive to use, so a critical border or a muted label meant the same thing everywhere. The goal was an interface that stayed quiet and out of the way, close to invisible. The brands and their content had to be the loudest thing on the platform.

![Design system tokens file with descriptive color tokens for text, icons, surfaces, tints, and borders, grouped by default, primary, critical, interactive, and state](/projects/marketfully/design-system.webp#wide "Tokens named by role, so a critical border or a muted label means the same thing on every screen.")

I also stood up and led the product design team: a Product Designer, an AI Solution Designer, and a UX Researcher, reporting to me. Together we owned how the product looked, how it behaved, and how we learned from the people using it. I worked on project definitions and the roadmap, and I managed a team of AI engineers and developers who turned those ideas and designs into features, alongside product, operations, and the business.

## What shipped: a workspace that holds the team and the brand

Organizations held workspaces and teams. Admins created teams, assigned each member a role such as account manager or member, and decided which teams and users could access each workspace. One company could run marketing, sales, and HR in parallel without splitting the brand across tools.

The brand lived in the product, not in a PDF. Every asset was part of the knowledge base behind each generation, and nothing entered it unchecked: an audience the AI proposed stayed suggested until someone iterated on it and approved it. Content for Argentina and content for the United States came from the same sources, under the same brief, adapted for different audiences, and checked for a possible negative effect through an AI review.

## From an opportunity to assets for every market

The value proposition came down to four pillars:

1. **Expert services.** Fully human workflows.
2. **Creation and transcreation.** Briefs and assets, with AI and people working on the same piece.
3. **Proactive suggestions.** Content ideas drawn from the brand's recent activity.
4. **Opportunities.** A system that ranked ideas by score, type, cost, market, and effort.

![Marketfully home with four creation paths labeled AI or human, and content suggested for a workspace](/projects/marketfully/home.webp#wide "Four pillars: expert services, creation and transcreation, suggestions from recent activity, and ranked opportunities.")

Every row in Opportunities ended in the same action: start a brief. The brief held the goal, audience, language and location, tone, and an outline per asset template. The assistant filled it in with the team, checked it for inconsistencies, and scored how complete it was before anything was generated. The draft then opened in the editor, beside the brief, its keywords, and a risk score.

Generation was a starting draft. The team iterated it in place, together: improving, shortening, checking keywords, adjusting the tone, or rewriting, without leaving the document. When the piece needed a native eye, they requested expert services: content review, accuracy verification, translation, or an SEO check, each tracked by cost and status. Once approved, the asset could be transcreated for other audiences, so a campaign could reach each market without being rewritten from scratch in every language, when that was the right path.

## What we agreed to change

We replaced the initial hypothesis with a tool marketing teams could actually run. Full automation was easy to sell, and in practice it was an illusion. A person in the lead was what worked. The agent drafts and reviews from brand data and outside context. The team, with AI in the process, reviews each change, and only then does the asset go out. An asset that drifted from its brief was a failed asset, even if an expert could have fixed it later.

> We replaced full automation with a person in the lead.

## What I learned

<Facts>
  <Fact title="Automation is not autonomy">The models saved time on most steps, but someone still had to guide production and approve the assets.</Fact>
  <Fact title="Document the brand once">When guidelines, tone, and glossaries live in the product, AI and people work from the same context.</Fact>
  <Fact title="Bind the asset to the brief">Generated work stays on-brand only if the brief stays attached while it is edited and iterated, and only if that brief was already approved.</Fact>
</Facts>
